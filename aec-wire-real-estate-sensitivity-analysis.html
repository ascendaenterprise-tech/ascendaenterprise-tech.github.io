Downside Framework

The downside case belongs inside the underwriting.

Real estate projections depend on assumptions that can change after acquisition. Sensitivity analysis helps identify which variables have the greatest effect on property economics and how much adverse movement an acquisition thesis may be able to absorb.

The objective is not to predict one exact downside scenario. It is to understand the range of outcomes created when important assumptions move away from the base case.

Occupancy & Revenue

Stress the income supporting the property.

Occupancy, rent levels, collections, concessions and tenant retention can diverge from initial expectations. Underwriting can test lower occupancy, slower leasing, weaker rent growth or other adverse revenue conditions to determine how dependent projected economics are on the base case.

For properties with concentrated tenant exposure, the effect of a major vacancy or lease expiration may deserve separate analysis.

Expense Growth

Test what happens when costs rise faster than expected.

Taxes, insurance, utilities, maintenance, management and other operating expenses can materially affect net operating income. Sensitivity analysis can evaluate the effect of higher costs rather than assuming historical expense levels remain constant.

The interaction between weaker revenue and higher expenses can be more important than either variable in isolation.

Capital Expenditures

Model cost overruns and timing risk.

Renovations, deferred maintenance, tenant improvements and unexpected physical work can require more capital or more time than initially estimated. Downside scenarios can incorporate larger budgets, delayed completion and contingency requirements.

This helps distinguish the stated purchase price from the potential total capital required to execute the acquisition plan.

Financing Stress

Debt can amplify changes in the operating case.

Interest rates, refinancing conditions, leverage, amortization and debt-service requirements influence equity risk. Sensitivity analysis can test higher borrowing costs, weaker refinancing conditions or reduced operating income against the proposed debt structure.

A property that remains viable without aggressive financing assumptions may have a different risk profile from one that requires favorable credit conditions to support the thesis.

Exit Sensitivity

Test terminal value instead of assuming appreciation.

Projected exit value can change significantly when capitalization rates, operating income or market liquidity change. Evaluating multiple exit capitalization rates and operating scenarios helps reveal how much of the expected outcome depends on favorable terminal assumptions.

A higher exit capitalization rate combined with weaker property income can create a materially different result from the base case.

Scenario Analysis

Combine variables instead of stressing them only one at a time.

Single-variable sensitivity tests help isolate individual drivers, while scenario analysis can combine multiple changes into coherent base, adverse and more severe cases. For example, weaker occupancy may occur alongside higher expenses, additional capital requirements or less favorable financing.

Combined scenarios can expose vulnerabilities that are difficult to see when each assumption is evaluated independently.

Decision Thresholds

Use sensitivity analysis to identify fragility.

A downside framework can help determine which variables most threaten the acquisition thesis and where additional diligence may be required. The goal is not to make uncertainty disappear but to determine whether the potential downside remains acceptable relative to the opportunity being evaluated.

Material assumptions should remain documented so later results can be compared with the original underwriting case.
