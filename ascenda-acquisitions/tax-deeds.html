Tax sale properties

Georgia tax sales, explained

Counties sell properties when owners fall behind on property taxes. Some sell far below market value, but the process has real risks. Here is how it works and how we approach it.

Get on the listCurrent deals

How it works

From tax lien to deed

Procedures differ by county. Always confirm with the county tax commissioner or sheriff.

01

The county posts a sale list

Georgia tax sales are normally held on the first Tuesday of the month. Counties publish the properties and opening bids ahead of time.

02

Bidders register and bring certified funds

The opening bid is the back taxes, interest, penalties and costs. The winning bidder pays in full, usually that day.

03

The buyer receives a tax deed

The deed is recorded with the county clerk. Title is not clean yet.

04

The owner has 12 months to redeem

If the former owner pays back the buyer plus a 20% premium in the first year, they keep the property and the buyer is repaid.

05

The buyer cuts off the right of redemption

After the year, an attorney gives the legal notices. A quiet title action is often needed so a title company will insure a resale.

Our approach

What we check before we bid

Value versus total cost

We compare estimated market value against the bid, legal costs, taxes, insurance and holding time.

Liens and claims

We look for IRS and state tax liens, code enforcement, HOA dues and mortgages that could survive the sale.

Condition and occupancy

We view the property from the street and skip anything we cannot confirm is worth the risk.

A written maximum bid

We set a limit per property in advance and do not go over it.

Risks

What to know before you consider tax sales

Do tax sale buyers get clean title right away?

No. In Georgia the former owner can redeem for a year, and a lawsuit is often needed to clear title.

Can I lose my money?

If the owner redeems you are repaid plus the premium, but your money is tied up. If title problems or liens appear, you can lose money. Costs are real.

Is this a way to buy a house for a few hundred dollars?

Not reliably. Opening bids are often low, but competition, legal costs and the redemption period change the math.

Is this investment advice?

No. This page is general education, not legal, tax or investment advice. Talk to a Georgia attorney.
